# 4. Design participation and belonging

A community is not created simply by bringing people together.

It develops when people:

- interact with one another
- feel that they belong
- see value in participating
- contribute their experience
- learn from others
- develop relationships
- gradually take ownership of the community

Your job as a community builder is therefore not to maximise attendance.

It is to create **meaningful opportunities for participation**.

---

# 4.1 Design for different kinds of participation

Not everyone will participate in the same way.

Some members may:

- read discussions
- attend events
- ask questions
- answer questions
- share resources
- introduce other members
- organise activities
- mentor others
- lead projects
- shape community priorities

All of these can be valuable.

Do not assume that the most visible members are the only valuable members.

---

# The participation pathway

Think about participation as a range of possibilities:

**Observe → Ask → Contribute → Collaborate → Lead**

### Observe

A member learns about the community and its culture.

Examples:

- reading a discussion
- attending an event
- exploring resources
- following community updates

### Ask

A member begins using the community to seek knowledge or experience.

Examples:

- asking a technical question
- seeking advice
- requesting recommendations
- asking whether someone has encountered a similar problem

### Contribute

A member begins sharing their own knowledge and experience.

Examples:

- answering a question
- sharing a resource
- presenting an experience
- documenting a solution
- introducing another member

### Collaborate

Members work together around a shared problem or activity.

Examples:

- developing a resource
- organising training
- working on a research project
- developing a protocol
- running a working group

### Lead

Members begin taking responsibility for the community itself.

Examples:

- facilitating discussions
- organising events
- mentoring others
- coordinating a working group
- representing the community
- helping set priorities

# 4.2 Create a welcoming first experience

The first interaction can strongly influence whether someone feels that they belong.

Consider the experience of a new member.

### Before they join

Do they understand:

- why the community exists?
- who it is for?
- what they can expect?
- what they can contribute?
- how much time participation requires?

### When they join

Do they know:

- where to start?
- who to contact?
- where conversations happen?
- what activities are available?
- how to introduce themselves?

### After joining

Do they receive an opportunity to:

- meet other members?
- ask a low-risk question?
- contribute something small?
- find something useful quickly?

# 4.3 Build belonging

People are more likely to contribute when they feel that they are part of something.

Belonging can develop through:

- personal introductions
- informal conversations
- peer relationships
- shared experiences
- recognition of contributions
- welcoming newcomers
- inclusive language
- opportunities to meet outside formal activities
- celebrating community achievements

Community-building is not only about exchanging information.

**Relationships are part of the value of the community.**

# 4.4 Make participation psychologically safer

Scientific communities often contain differences in:

- seniority
- institutional power
- expertise
- geography
- language
- access to resources
- confidence

These differences can influence who speaks and who remains silent.

Design participation so that people can contribute without feeling that they need to demonstrate expertise every time.

Possible approaches include:

- small-group discussions
- anonymous questions
- peer discussion before plenary discussion
- rotating facilitators
- explicitly inviting quieter voices
- creating beginner-friendly activities
- making it acceptable to say "I don't know"
- recognising different forms of expertise

# 4.5 Avoid designing everything around events

Events can be useful, but a community should not disappear between events.

Think about the full engagement ecosystem.

| Activity | Purpose | Frequency |

| Peer problem-solving | | |

| Seminar/webinar | | |
| Workshop | | |
| Informal social activity | | |
| Working group | | |
| Mentoring | | |
| Collaborative project | | |

The aim is not to fill everyone's calendar.

The aim is to create a **sustainable rhythm of meaningful interaction**.

---

# 4.6 Design for contribution, not consumption

A common failure mode is:

> Organisation produces content → members consume content → organisation produces more content.

This creates an audience.

A Community of Practice should increasingly become:

> Members share → members discuss → members create → members learn → members lead.

Ask of every planned activity:

> **Where is the member contribution?**

For example:

### Instead of:

> "We will run a webinar about genome assembly."

Consider:

> "We will invite three members to share genome assembly problems they have encountered, followed by peer troubleshooting."

The second design creates an opportunity for members to contribute experience and learn from one another.

---

# 4.7 Recognise contribution

People need to know that their contributions matter.

Recognition does not necessarily mean prizes or formal awards.

It can be:

- thanking someone publicly
- highlighting a useful contribution
- inviting someone to lead an activity
- giving members visibility
- sharing their work
- acknowledging expertise
- inviting them to mentor others

Ask:

> **How will we notice and recognise contribution?**
