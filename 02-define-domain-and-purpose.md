# 2. Define your domain and purpose

A strong community starts with a strong reason to exist.

Before deciding on platforms, events, branding or membership numbers, define:

1. **What is the community's domain?**
2. **What do members want to achieve or learn together?**
3. **Why is a community the right way to do this?**

A Community of Practice is built around a shared domain, a community of people who interact around that domain, and a developing shared practice.

The three should evolve together.

---

## 2.1 Define the domain

The **domain** is the area of knowledge, practice or challenge that gives the community its identity.

It should be specific enough that people recognise what connects them, but broad enough to allow learning and development.

### Examples

| Too broad | Better defined |
|---|---|
| Genomics | Genomic surveillance for antimicrobial resistance |
| Bioinformatics | Genome sequence analysis for public health |
| AI | AI applications in genomics |
| Science | Scientific community engagement |
| Training | Training and mentorship for genomics practitioners |

### Ask yourself

> What specific area of practice brings these people together?

Write your first draft:

**Our domain is:**

> ...

---

## 2.2 Identify the shared challenge

A domain alone is not enough.

Ask:

> What problem, opportunity or challenge do members have in common?

For example:

- We need to strengthen genomic surveillance capacity across our region.
- We need to share approaches for training bioinformatics practitioners.
- We need to develop better ways of applying AI to genomic data.
- We need to connect researchers working on similar technical problems.
- We need to develop and share resources that individual teams do not have the capacity to create alone.

### Complete the sentence

> People in our domain currently struggle with...

> ...

---

## 2.3 Define the collective value

Now ask:

> **What becomes possible because these people are connected?**

A useful community provides value that members could not easily obtain alone.

Potential forms of collective value include:

### Learning

Members learn from one another through experience, discussion and peer exchange.

### Problem solving

Members bring real problems to the community and work through them together.

### Knowledge sharing

Members share resources, methods, tools, experiences and lessons learned.

### Collaboration

Members identify opportunities to work together on research, training or other activities.

### Capacity building

Members help develop skills and confidence within the community.

### Innovation

Members experiment with new approaches and develop new practices.

### Collective voice

Members can identify common challenges and develop a shared perspective.

---

## Activity: The "because we're together" test

Complete this sentence:

> **If these people were not connected, they would struggle to...**

> ...

Now complete:

> **Because they are connected, they can...**

> ...

The second statement should describe something meaningful that emerges from the community.

---

# 2.4 Define the purpose

Your purpose should describe **why the community exists**, rather than simply listing what activities it will organise.

### Weak

> We will organise webinars and meetings about genomics.

This describes activities.

### Stronger

> We bring together genomics practitioners across Africa to exchange experience, strengthen local capacity and develop collaborative approaches to genomic surveillance.

This describes purpose.

---

## Purpose formula

Try:

> **We bring together [WHO] around [DOMAIN] to [SHARED PURPOSE].**

For example:

> We bring together bioinformatics practitioners across Southern Africa around genomic surveillance for antimicrobial resistance to share expertise, strengthen regional capacity and develop collaborative solutions.

Write yours:

> **We bring together...**

> ...

---

# 2.5 Define 3–5 objectives

Once the purpose is clear, identify a small number of objectives.

Good objectives describe what the community wants to achieve through collective action.

Examples:

- Facilitate peer-to-peer learning.
- Share practical approaches and resources.
- Develop the skills of emerging practitioners.
- Create opportunities for collaboration.
- Identify and address common challenges.
- Develop shared training resources.
- Support mentorship.
- Strengthen connections between institutions.
- Develop locally relevant solutions.
- Create opportunities for members to contribute to research, training or policy.

# 2.6 Don't over-engineer the purpose

Your first version does not need to be perfect.

Communities change.

As members interact, you may discover:

- new priorities
- new expertise
- unexpected collaborations
- gaps in the original purpose
- new opportunities
- activities that are not creating value

The purpose should therefore provide direction without becoming a rigid contract.

Review it periodically.

---

# 2.7 Check your purpose

Before moving on, ask:

| Question | Yes | Not yet |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| Is the domain clear? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Would potential members recognise themselves in it? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Is there a meaningful shared challenge? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Is there a reason to work collectively? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Does the purpose describe value rather than activities? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Can members see how they could contribute? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Is the purpose broad enough to evolve? | ☐ | ☐ |

If several answers are "Not yet", go back and refine the previous sections.
