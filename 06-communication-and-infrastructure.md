# 6. Choose communication and infrastructure

Technology should serve the community — not define it.

A Community of Practice does not become a community because it has a website, Slack workspace, WhatsApp group or discussion forum.

The right question is:

> **What does our community need to do, and what is the simplest infrastructure that will support it?**

Start with the community's behaviours and needs.

Choose technology afterwards.

---

# 6.1 Start with the activities

Think back to your participation and engagement plans.

What does your community actually need to do?

For example:

- have ongoing discussions
- ask technical questions
- share resources
- organise events
- collaborate on documents
- share code
- announce opportunities
- mentor one another
- run working groups
- hold virtual meetings
- maintain a knowledge base
- coordinate projects

Different activities may require different tools.

# 6.2 Give every platform a clear job

If you use multiple platforms, each should have a clear purpose.

For example:

| Platform | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Email | Important announcements |
| Messaging platform | Quick interaction and informal conversation |
| GitHub | Code, documentation and collaborative technical resources |
| Video platform | Virtual meetings |
| Shared drive | Working documents |
| Community website | Public-facing information and resources |

These are examples, not recommendations.

The tools should reflect your members' actual needs and existing habits.

---

# 6.3 Avoid platform overload

More platforms do not necessarily mean more engagement.

A member should not have to remember:

> "The webinar is on one platform, questions are on another, resources are on a third, the working group uses a fourth, and important announcements arrive by email."

Every additional platform creates:

- another account
- another notification stream
- another place to check
- another place for information to become fragmented

Ask:

> **Can we achieve the same outcome with fewer tools?**

If yes, simplify.

---

# 6.4 Choose your primary community space

Most communities benefit from having one place that members recognise as:

> **"This is where our community happens."**

This might be:

- a discussion forum
- Slack
- Discord
- Microsoft Teams
- a mailing list
- a professional network
- another platform already used by members

Choose based on:

- member preferences
- accessibility
- institutional restrictions
- geographical context
- connectivity
- privacy
- moderation requirements
- sustainability
- cost
- technical complexity

# 6.5 Make communication purposeful

A communication channel needs a reason for existing.

Before creating a channel, ask:

> **What behaviour do we want to happen here?**

For example:

### Technical discussion

> Members ask questions and help one another solve problems.

### Opportunities

> Members share relevant training, funding, jobs and collaboration opportunities.

### Introductions

> New members introduce themselves and find connections.

### Resources

> Members share useful tools, papers, protocols and guides.

### Social

> Members build informal relationships beyond technical discussions.

# 6.6 Establish communication norms

Members need to understand how communication works.

Consider:

### Response expectations

How quickly should questions normally receive a response?

> 

### Tone

What tone should members use?

> 

### Language

Will the community use one language or multiple languages?

> 

### Confidentiality

What information should not be shared outside the community?

> 

### Attribution

How should members be credited when their ideas or resources are shared?

> 

### Self-promotion

What types of promotion are appropriate?

> 

### Moderation

Who handles inappropriate or harmful content?

>

# 6.7 Make information findable

A community can generate enormous amounts of useful knowledge.

But information that cannot be found again has limited value.

Think about how members will find:

- previous discussions
- useful resources
- recordings
- training materials
- protocols
- FAQs
- documentation
- shared outputs
- decisions
- opportunities

Ask:

> **If a member asks a question that was answered six months ago, can they find the answer?**

If not, consider creating a knowledge structure.

---

# 6.8 Build a shared knowledge base

Your community's practice can become visible through shared artefacts.

These might include:

- guides
- protocols
- FAQs
- case studies
- training materials
- code
- datasets
- templates
- recorded sessions
- lessons learned
- troubleshooting guides
- recommendations

The objective is not to document everything.

Instead ask:

> **What knowledge would be useful to preserve and reuse?**

# 6.9 Make the infrastructure accessible

Scientific communities may span countries, institutions and levels of connectivity.

Consider:

- bandwidth
- mobile access
- accessibility needs
- institutional firewalls
- blocked platforms
- data costs
- language
- time zones
- device availability
- software requirements

A platform that works perfectly for one group may be inaccessible to another.

### Ask:

> **Who could be excluded by our technology choices?**

> 

> **What could we change to reduce that barrier?**

> 

---

# 6.10 Do not make the community dependent on one platform

Platforms change.

Policies change.

Organisations change.

People leave.

Build infrastructure that allows your community to retain its knowledge and relationships even if a particular tool changes.

Consider:

- maintaining an up-to-date member database
- documenting important processes
- backing up key resources
- using portable formats
- keeping ownership of important community assets clear
- documenting who has administrative access
- maintaining a list of recurring activities and contacts

---

# 6.11 Assign ownership

Every important piece of infrastructure should have someone responsible for it.

This does not mean one person must do all the work.

It means someone knows:

> **Who is responsible for making sure this works?**

---

# 6.12 Create a minimum viable infrastructure

You do not need to build everything before launch.

Start with the minimum infrastructure needed for the community's first activities.

For example:

### Before launch

- a way to contact members
- a place for community information
- a way to communicate
- a way to organise the first activity
- a place to store important resources

### Later

Add infrastructure when a real need emerges.

> **Build for today's behaviour, not tomorrow's imagined community.**

## Sources & evidence

### Primary CoP source

Wenger-Trayner and Wenger-Trayner distinguish Communities of Practice from simply having a website, forum or network. Technology can support a community, but the community exists through interaction, learning and the development of shared practice.

**Reference:**  
Wenger-Trayner, E. & Wenger-Trayner, B. (2015). *Introduction to communities of practice: A brief overview of the concept and its uses.*

[Read the original introduction →](https://www.wenger-trayner.com/introduction-to-communities-of-practice/)

### Additional source

Robinson's research on collaborative networks highlights the importance of sufficient infrastructure and coordination while cautioning against unnecessary formalisation. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

**Reference:**  
Robinson, A. (2012). *The Less Visible Leader: Emerging Leadership Models for Environmental Networks, Coalitions, and Collaboratives.*

### Our synthesis

The platform test, communication mapping exercise and minimum-viable-infrastructure approach were developed specifically for this guide.

They are intended to help scientific communities choose tools according to their actual practices rather than reproducing a particular technology or platform framework.
