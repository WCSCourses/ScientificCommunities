# 5. Establish leadership and governance

A Community of Practice needs leadership.

But leadership in a community is different from managing a conventional team.

Members may come from different institutions, countries, career stages and professional backgrounds. They may not report to the community coordinator and may have different priorities.

The question is therefore not:

> **"How do we manage our members?"**

It is:

> **"How do we create the conditions for members to contribute, make decisions and take ownership?"**

Effective community leadership combines **coordination with shared ownership**.

---

# 5.1 Start with enough structure

A community does not need a complicated constitution before it can begin.

But it does need enough infrastructure to function.

Members should understand:

- who coordinates the community
- how decisions are made
- how activities are organised
- how members can contribute
- how conflicts are handled
- how information is shared
- how new leaders can emerge

The goal is:

> **Enough structure to help people work together, but not so much structure that it prevents participation and experimentation.**

---

# 5.2 Decide what needs to be governed

Not every decision needs formal governance.

Start by identifying the decisions that matter.

### Strategic direction

Who decides:

- the community's priorities?
- major changes to its purpose?
- new areas of activity?

### Programming

Who decides:

- what activities happen?
- who facilitates them?
- what topics are prioritised?

### Membership

Who can participate?

Is the community:

- open to everyone?
- open to people meeting certain criteria?
- invitation-based?
- a combination of these?

### Resources

Who manages:

- funding?
- shared resources?
- community platforms?
- data?
- intellectual property?

### Representation

Who represents the community externally?

### Conflict

What happens when members disagree?

# 5.3 Choose a leadership model

There is no single correct model.

Consider which of these best fits your community.

### Founder/coordinator-led

One person or organisation provides most of the coordination.

**Useful when:**

- the community is new
- someone needs to establish basic infrastructure
- there is a clear sponsoring organisation

**Risk:**

The community becomes dependent on one person.

---

### Steering group

A small group shares responsibility for direction and decisions.

**Useful when:**

- the community spans several institutions
- decisions need broader representation
- there is enough activity to justify shared governance

**Risk:**

The steering group can become disconnected from ordinary members.

---

### Distributed leadership

Different members lead different activities, projects or areas of practice.

**Useful when:**

- members have strong expertise
- the community is mature
- there are many opportunities for contribution

**Risk:**

Responsibilities can become unclear without good coordination.

---

### Hybrid model

A coordinator or small team provides infrastructure while members lead activities and shape priorities.

This can provide a useful balance between:

**coordination + member ownership**

# 5.4 Define the coordinator's role

The coordinator is not necessarily "the leader" of everything.

Think of the coordinator as someone who helps the community function.

Possible responsibilities include:

- maintaining communication channels
- organising meetings
- welcoming new members
- connecting people
- supporting member-led activities
- documenting decisions
- identifying emerging needs
- monitoring community health
- maintaining the community calendar
- supporting facilitators
- helping resolve problems
- ensuring follow-up

Some of the most important work may be invisible.

Good coordination creates the conditions for other people to succeed.

---

# The coordinator should ask:

> **What does the community need from me?**

rather than:

> **What can I do for the community?**

The distinction matters.

The first question focuses attention on the system and its members.

---

# 5.5 Create space for members to lead

A healthy community should not require everything to pass through the coordinator.

Look for opportunities to transfer responsibility.

For example:

### Instead of the coordinator organising every event:

Invite members to propose and host sessions.

### Instead of the coordinator answering every question:

Connect members who can help one another.

### Instead of the coordinator deciding every priority:

Ask members to identify and rank priorities.

### Instead of the coordinator producing every resource:

Create collaborative working groups.

---

# The "step back" test

Ask yourself:

> **If I disappeared for one month, what would stop happening?**

List everything:

> ...

Now ask:

> **What could someone else take ownership of?**

> ...

Then:

> **What infrastructure would they need from me?**

> ...

Your goal is not to make yourself unnecessary immediately.

Your goal is to gradually increase the community's ability to function without depending on one person.

# 5.7 Decide how decisions are made

Different decisions may require different approaches.

### Consensus

Useful when:

- relationships are important
- decisions affect everyone
- there is time for discussion

### Consultation

The coordinator or leadership group makes the decision after seeking member input.

Useful when:

- decisions need to be made efficiently
- specialist knowledge is required

### Delegation

A member or working group is given responsibility for a decision.

Useful when:

- the decision relates to a specific area of expertise
- ownership will increase participation

### Vote

Useful when:

- a clear decision is required
- consensus cannot be reached
- the community has agreed that voting is appropriate

Do not create elaborate decision-making processes before you need them.

# 5.8 Make power visible

Scientific communities can contain significant power differences.

Consider differences in:

- seniority
- institutional status
- funding
- geographical location
- access to equipment
- expertise
- language
- professional networks

Power can affect who speaks, whose ideas are taken seriously and who gets opportunities.

Ask:

> **Who currently has the most influence in our community?**

> ...

> **Who may have less influence?**

> ...

> **What could we do to distribute opportunities more fairly?**

> ...

Possible approaches include:

- rotating facilitation
- ensuring geographical representation
- creating opportunities for early-career members
- inviting quieter voices
- transparent selection processes
- rotating leadership roles
- making opportunities visible to all members
- separating expertise from decision-making authority where appropriate

---

# 5.9 Establish expectations for behaviour

A community needs a shared understanding of how people interact.

Do not create rules simply because rules are expected.

Focus on behaviours that support learning and participation.

For example:

### We will:

- treat members with respect
- assume good intentions while addressing harmful behaviour
- welcome different perspectives
- make space for quieter voices
- give credit for contributions
- be open about conflicts of interest
- respect confidentiality where appropriate
- challenge ideas without attacking people
- acknowledge when we do not know something
- encourage constructive disagreement

### We will not:

- tolerate harassment or discrimination
- dismiss people because of seniority or status
- claim others' work as our own
- use the community primarily for self-promotion
- allow a small group to dominate every discussion

Adapt these principles to your own context and organisational requirements.

---

# 5.10 Plan for leadership transitions

People leave communities.

Coordinators change jobs.

Funding changes.

Institutions change priorities.

Design for continuity from the beginning.

Ask:

> Who knows how the community works?

> ...

> Where is important information stored?

> ...

> What happens if the coordinator leaves?

> ...

> How can someone else take over?

> ...

> Which responsibilities need documentation?

> ...

Useful documentation might include:

- governance arrangements
- member lists
- communication channels
- recurring activities
- meeting templates
- passwords/access arrangements
- key contacts
- funding information
- partnership agreements
- community history
- lessons learned

---

# 5.11 Governance should evolve

Your governance model at launch does not need to be your governance model forever.

As the community grows, you may need:

**More structure**

when:

- membership grows
- activities multiply
- decisions become more complex
- resources increase
- external representation becomes important

**Less centralisation**

when:

- members are taking more initiative
- working groups emerge
- leadership is distributed
- the coordinator becomes a bottleneck

Review governance periodically.

## Sources & influences

This section draws substantially on Andy Robinson's analysis of collaborative and network leadership in *The Less Visible Leader*, particularly the principles of shared power, creating space for others to contribute, building trust, embracing ambiguity, providing sufficient infrastructure, resolving differences and adapting over time.

The section also draws on Communities of Practice literature and the **Scientific Community Engagement Fundamentals** course from the Center for Scientific Collaboration and Community Engagement (CSCCE).

### Key sources

- Robinson, A. (2012). *The Less Visible Leader: Emerging Leadership Models for Environmental Networks, Coalitions, and Collaboratives*. Institute for Conservation Leadership.
- Wenger-Trayner, E. & Wenger-Trayner, B. (2015). *Introduction to communities of practice: A brief overview of the concept and its uses.*
- Center for Scientific Collaboration and Community Engagement (CSCCE). *Scientific Community Engagement Fundamentals.*

**Adaptation:** Robinson's work focuses primarily on environmental networks, coalitions and collaboratives rather than scientific Communities of Practice. The principles have therefore been adapted here to the context of scientific CoPs.

**Our synthesis:** The governance canvas, "step-back test" and decision-making exercises were developed for this guide.
