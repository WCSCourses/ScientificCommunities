# 8. Facilitate meaningful community activities

A Community of Practice develops through what members **do together**.

A mailing list, discussion forum or calendar of events can support a community, but they are not the community itself.

The most valuable activities create opportunities for members to:

- share experience
- solve problems together
- learn from one another
- develop relationships
- create shared resources
- reflect on their practice
- experiment with new approaches
- contribute to the development of the community

The goal is not to organise more events.

The goal is to create **useful interactions around the community's shared practice**.

---

# 8.1 Start with the need, not the activity

Before deciding what activity to run, ask:

> **What does the community need right now?**

For example:

### Members need to learn something

→ Learning session

### Members have a shared problem

→ Peer problem-solving session

### Members want to share experience

→ Member showcase

### Members need to develop something together

→ Working group

### Members need advice from peers

→ Case clinic

### Members are building relationships

→ Informal community gathering

### Members need ongoing support

→ Mentoring or peer-support group

Choose the activity based on the need.

# 8.2 Design activities around participation

A common mistake is to design activities where:

> One person speaks → everyone else listens.

That can be useful for information delivery, but it does not necessarily create a Community of Practice.

Consider how you can shift from:

**presentation → interaction**

**expert → peer exchange**

**content delivery → problem-solving**

**organiser-led → member-led**

For example:

### Instead of:

> A 60-minute lecture about a bioinformatics method.

Try:

> A 15-minute overview followed by participants bringing real problems and comparing approaches.

The second format creates much more opportunity for members to learn from one another.

---

# 8.3 Activity format: Case clinic

A case clinic allows a member to bring a real problem to the community.

### Structure

**1. Present the case — 5–10 minutes**

The member explains:

- the context
- the problem
- what they have tried
- where they are stuck

**2. Clarifying questions — 5 minutes**

Participants ask questions to understand the situation.

**3. Peer discussion — 15–30 minutes**

Participants discuss:

- possible approaches
- relevant experience
- resources
- alternative perspectives

**4. Reflection — 5 minutes**

The person who presented identifies:

- useful ideas
- possible next steps
- questions that remain

### Good for:

- technical problems
- implementation challenges
- teaching challenges
- research practice
- leadership questions
- community-building challenges

---

# 8.4 Activity format: Peer problem-solving

Bring members together around a common challenge.

### Structure

**1. Define the challenge**

> What are we trying to solve?

**2. Share experiences**

> What have people already tried?

**3. Compare approaches**

> What worked? What did not?

**4. Identify possibilities**

> What could we try next?

**5. Capture learning**

> What should the community remember?

The final step is important.

A useful discussion should ideally leave behind something that can be reused.

---

# 8.5 Activity format: Member showcase

Invite members to share something they are doing.

This could be:

- a research approach
- a tool
- a training initiative
- a community project
- a lesson learned
- an unexpected failure
- a useful resource

Keep presentations short.

For example:

> **10 minutes:** Member shares  
> **10 minutes:** Questions  
> **10 minutes:** Discussion

The objective is not to create a conference presentation.

It is to create a conversation between practitioners.

---

# 8.6 Activity format: Learning circle

A learning circle brings a small group together around a shared topic.

Members might:

1. agree on a question
2. bring relevant resources or experiences
3. discuss what they are learning
4. test ideas in their own context
5. return and share what happened

This works particularly well when the community wants to explore something that cannot be learned through a single workshop.

# 8.7 Activity format: Working group

Use a working group when members need to **create something together**.

Possible outputs include:

- a guide
- protocol
- training resource
- software
- curriculum
- community standard
- recommendation
- shared dataset
- position statement

Before creating a working group, define:

### Problem

> 

### Desired output

> 

### Members needed

> 

### Decision-making process

> 

### Timeline

> 

### Coordinator/facilitator

> 

### How the wider community will contribute

> 

Avoid creating working groups simply because they sound productive.

There should be a clear reason for them to exist.

# 8.8 Activity format: Peer mentoring

Peer mentoring can help members learn from people facing similar challenges.

It can be:

- one-to-one
- small-group
- reciprocal
- time-limited
- ongoing

The key is that mentoring should not always be:

> Expert → novice

Peer mentoring can also be:

> Practitioner ↔ practitioner

where both people bring experience and learn from one another.

---

# 8.9 Activity format: Resource exchange

Invite members to share:

- protocols
- papers
- software
- datasets
- teaching materials
- funding opportunities
- useful contacts
- tools
- examples of good practice

But avoid creating a passive resource library.

For each resource, encourage members to add:

> **Why is this useful?**

> **How have you used it?**

> **What should others know before using it?**

Context makes shared resources much more valuable.

---

# 8.10 Activity format: Reflection session

Learning does not only happen through successful activities.

Invite members to reflect on:

- what they tried
- what happened
- what worked
- what failed
- what they would change
- what others could learn

This can be particularly useful in scientific practice because members often learn as much from unsuccessful approaches as successful ones.

---

# 8.11 Create a rhythm, not a rigid calendar

A healthy community benefits from some predictability.

For example:

### Weekly

Informal discussion or peer questions

### Monthly

Member-led activity

### Quarterly

Larger community gathering

### As needed

Working groups or problem-solving sessions

But do not create activities simply to fill the calendar.

Ask:

> **Is this interaction creating value?**

If not, change it.

---

# 8.12 Rotate facilitation

If the same person facilitates every activity, members may begin to see the community as belonging to the organiser.

Where appropriate, rotate facilitation.

A facilitator does not need to be the most senior or knowledgeable person.

Their role is to:

- frame the discussion
- make participation possible
- keep the group focused
- draw out different perspectives
- manage time
- capture useful learning
- help the group reach its next step

---

# 8.13 Capture the practice

A Community of Practice develops knowledge through interaction.

Some of that knowledge should become visible.

After an activity, consider capturing:

### What did we discuss?

> 

### What did we learn?

> 

### What approaches were suggested?

> 

### What remains unresolved?

> 

### What resources were shared?

> 

### What should happen next?

> 

Do not attempt to document everything.

Capture the things that are likely to be useful again.

# 8.15 Activity health check

Afterwards, ask:

| Question | Yes | Not yet |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| Did members contribute? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Did participants learn from one another? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Was the activity connected to the shared practice? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Did different perspectives emerge? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Did members build relationships? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Was something useful created or learned? | ☐ | ☐ |
| Is there a reason to continue the interaction? | ☐ | ☐ |

If most answers are "Not yet", change the format rather than simply repeating the same activity.
