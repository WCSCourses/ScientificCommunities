# 7. Launch the community

You have now defined:

- why the community should exist
- its domain and purpose
- who it is for
- how people can participate
- how leadership will work
- what infrastructure you need

Now it is time to bring people together.

But don't think of the launch as a single event.

A successful Community of Practice begins developing **before** the launch and continues evolving afterwards.

> **The launch is a transition into community life — not the moment the community suddenly comes into existence.**

---

# 7.1 Start before you launch

Before announcing the community widely, talk to potential members.

You want to find out:

- Do they recognise the challenge?
- Does the proposed purpose matter to them?
- Do they see value in learning with others?
- What would they want to work on?
- What could they contribute?
- What would make participation difficult?
- Who might help shape the community?

These conversations are also an opportunity to avoid designing the community entirely from the organiser's perspective.

Wenger-Trayner and Wenger-Trayner recommend conversations with potential members as an important part of getting a community started. They emphasise understanding people's challenges and finding potential members who are willing to invest in the community. 

---

# 7.2 Find your founding group

You do not need hundreds of members to begin.

Identify a small group of people who are genuinely interested in helping the community develop.

These people might:

- help refine the purpose
- suggest activities
- introduce other members
- facilitate the first discussion
- contribute resources
- host the first event
- help establish community norms
- provide feedback on the launch

They do not need to become a permanent committee.

Their role is to help the community get moving.

# 7.3 Decide whether you need a formal launch

Not every community needs a big launch event.

There are at least three possible approaches.

## Option A — Start with a problem

Bring a real challenge to a small group and invite people to work on it together.

For example:

> "A laboratory in our network is struggling with X. Has anyone dealt with something similar?"

This can be a powerful way to demonstrate the value of peer learning.

---

## Option B — Start with a small gathering

Bring potential members together for a discussion about:

- the shared challenge
- what they need
- what they could contribute
- what the community could become

Use the session to shape the community rather than simply presenting a finished plan.

---

## Option C — Formal launch event

A larger event can be useful when you already have:

- a defined group of potential members
- a clear purpose
- some initial relationships
- a reason for people to gather
- activities that create immediate value

A formal launch can create visibility and commitment.

But it should be the culmination of preparation, not the beginning of all community-building.

# 7.4 Design the first activity around a real challenge

Your first activity should demonstrate the community's value.

Avoid spending the entire first meeting:

- presenting the community structure
- explaining governance
- delivering long presentations
- talking about future activities

Give people an opportunity to **experience the community**.

---

## A useful first-session structure

### 1. Welcome

Explain briefly:

- why people are here
- the challenge that connects them
- what will happen during the session

### 2. Meet each other

Ask participants to introduce themselves through the lens of the shared practice.

For example:

> What are you currently working on?

> What is one challenge you are trying to solve?

### 3. Explore the shared challenge

Identify:

- common problems
- differences in context
- existing approaches
- gaps in knowledge

### 4. Work together

Use a case, question, problem or scenario that requires participants to share experience.

### 5. Reflect

Ask:

> What did we learn from each other?

> What was useful?

> What surprised us?

### 6. Shape the next step

Ask:

> What should we do together next?

---

# 7.5 Make the first contribution easy

People are more likely to participate when the first contribution is manageable.

Examples:

- answer a simple question
- introduce themselves
- share one useful resource
- describe one challenge
- comment on another person's idea
- vote on a topic
- volunteer to help with one small task

Don't immediately ask:

> "Who wants to lead a working group?"

Build contribution gradually.

---

# 7.6 Create a clear next step

Never end your first community interaction with:

> "Thanks everyone. We'll be in touch."

Instead, give people something concrete.

For example:

> "Next month we'll run a peer session on genome assembly. Two members have volunteered to facilitate."

Or:

> "We've identified three common challenges. We'll create small groups to explore them."

Or:

> "We'll continue the discussion online and meet again in four weeks."

The community needs momentum.

---

# 7.7 Follow up quickly

After the first activity:

### Within 24–48 hours

Thank participants and share:

- key takeaways
- useful resources
- agreed actions
- next activity

### Within one week

Contact people who volunteered to contribute.

### Within one month

Hold or announce the next meaningful interaction.

The objective is to turn:

**one successful interaction → an ongoing pattern of interaction.**

---

# 7.8 Your first 90 days

The first three months are a period of experimentation.

Do not try to establish every process immediately.

Focus on:

### Month 1 — Connect

**Goal:** establish relationships and demonstrate value.

Possible activities:

- launch discussion
- introductions
- first peer-learning session
- identify common challenges

### Month 2 — Contribute

**Goal:** encourage members to share knowledge and experience.

Possible activities:

- member-led session
- case clinic
- resource exchange
- peer problem-solving

### Month 3 — Co-create

**Goal:** move from organiser-led activity towards shared ownership.

Possible activities:

- working group
- collaborative resource
- member-led event
- mentoring initiative
- community project

# 7.9 Don't confuse attendance with success

A launch can attract 100 people and still fail to create a community.

Conversely, a small group of 12 people who regularly help each other may be developing something highly valuable.

Look for signs such as:

- members returning
- members asking questions
- members answering one another
- members sharing resources
- members introducing others
- members organising activities
- members collaborating
- relationships developing
- members identifying new opportunities
- responsibility becoming distributed

These behaviours tell you more than attendance alone.

---

# 7.10 Learn and adapt

Your initial design is a hypothesis.

After the first few interactions, ask:

### What created value?

> 

### What did members engage with?

> 

### What did they ignore?

> 

### What surprised us?

> 

### What are members asking for?

> 

### Who is participating?

> 

### Who is not participating?

> 

### What should we change?

> 

Don't be afraid to change the original plan.

A community becomes stronger by learning from its own experience.

### Primary source

Wenger-Trayner, E. & Wenger-Trayner, B. (2015). *Introduction to communities of practice: A brief overview of the concept and its uses.*

The authors recommend conversations with potential members, identifying people willing to invest in the community and involving a core group in designing how the community will get started. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

### Practical source

Wenger-Trayner, E., Wenger-Trayner, B., Reid, P. & Bruderlein, C. (2023/2024). *Communities of Practice within and across Organizations: A Guidebook.*

The guidebook discusses different approaches to launching, including starting through an informal social-learning experience rather than a formal launch event. It also emphasises that community-building begins before a launch event and that launch activities can create identity and commitment. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}
