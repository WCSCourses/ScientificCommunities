# 3. Understand your members

A community is only as useful as the value that members experience from participating.

One of the most common mistakes when establishing a Community of Practice is to start by asking:

> **"What activities should we organise?"**

Instead, start with:

> **"Who are our members, what do they need, and what can they contribute?"**

Your community is unlikely to contain one type of participant. People will have different levels of experience, different motivations and different amounts of time available.

Design for that diversity.

---

# 3.1 Identify your member groups

Start by identifying the different types of people you want in your community.

For a scientific CoP, these might include:

- early-career researchers
- experienced researchers
- bioinformaticians
- laboratory scientists
- clinicians
- trainers
- educators
- research leaders
- technical specialists
- institutional representatives
- policymakers
- industry partners
- community organisers

These are only examples.

Your member groups should reflect your own domain.

# 3.2 Understand motivation

People rarely participate simply because a community exists.

They participate because they expect something valuable from it.

Possible motivations include:

### Learning

> "I want to develop my skills."

### Problem solving

> "I have a problem and want advice from people who have dealt with it."

### Contribution

> "I have experience that could help others."

### Collaboration

> "I want to find people to work with."

### Connection

> "I want to build relationships with people working in my field."

### Recognition

> "I want my expertise and contribution to be visible."

### Career development

> "I want to develop my professional network and opportunities."

### Belonging

> "I want to be part of a community of people who understand the challenges I face."

### Influence

> "I want to help shape the direction of the field."

# 3.3 Identify member needs

Now consider what members actually need from the community.

Needs might include:

- access to expertise
- practical advice
- training
- mentoring
- collaboration opportunities
- visibility
- peer support
- resources
- career opportunities
- funding information
- technical troubleshooting
- opportunities to teach
- opportunities to lead
- access to new ideas

Avoid assuming that everyone needs the same thing.

# 3.4 Identify barriers to participation

Understanding what prevents participation is just as important as understanding what motivates it.

Common barriers might include:

- lack of time
- competing work priorities
- unclear value
- lack of confidence
- language barriers
- geographical distance
- poor internet connectivity
- inconvenient meeting times
- accessibility barriers
- lack of institutional support
- uncertainty about what contribution is expected
- fear of asking questions
- concerns about hierarchy or status
- previous negative experiences

> **What could make it difficult for each member group to participate?**

# 3.5 Design for different levels of participation

Not everyone needs to participate in the same way.

A healthy community can offer different ways to engage.

### Observe

Members may initially read, listen or attend without contributing publicly.

### Ask

Members begin asking questions and seeking advice.

### Contribute

Members share resources, experience, ideas or answers.

### Collaborate

Members work with others on projects, activities or shared outputs.

### Lead

Members organise activities, facilitate discussions, mentor others or shape community priorities.

These are not necessarily fixed stages.

A highly experienced member may be very active in one area and a passive learner in another.

# Example persona

### Early-career bioinformatician

**Who are they?**

An early-career scientist working with genomic data who has strong technical skills but limited access to experienced peers working on similar problems.

**Why might they join?**

To learn from others, troubleshoot problems and build connections beyond their institution.

**What do they need?**

Practical advice, peer support, mentoring and opportunities to develop their skills.

**What might they contribute?**

Technical knowledge, new tools, enthusiasm and experience with emerging methods.

**What might prevent participation?**

Limited time, uncertainty about whether their experience is valuable and reluctance to speak in a group dominated by senior scientists.

**What would make participation valuable?**

Being able to ask practical questions and receive useful answers from peers.

**What could they eventually lead?**

A discussion group, technical session, working group or mentoring activity.

# 3.7 Don't design only for your most active members

A common mistake is to design everything around the people who already participate most.

Your community should create pathways for:

- newcomers
- quieter members
- occasional participants
- experienced contributors
- emerging leaders

Think about how someone can move from:

**newcomer → participant → contributor → collaborator → community leader**

But remember:

> **Participation is not a ladder that everyone must climb.**

People should be able to contribute in ways that fit their interests, circumstances and availability.

---

# 3.8 Validate your assumptions

Everything you've written so far is a hypothesis.

Don't assume you know what members want.

Talk to them.

Useful approaches include:

- one-to-one conversations
- short surveys
- focus groups
- informal discussions
- observing existing community interactions
- analysing questions members already ask
- asking members to prioritise needs

### Questions you could ask

> What would make this community genuinely useful to you?

> What would make you come back?

> What would you want to learn from other members?

> What could you contribute?

> What would make participation difficult?

> What types of activities would you actually attend?

> What would make you comfortable contributing?

> What would you never want this community to become?

---

# Checkpoint

Before moving on, you should be able to answer:

- Who are our main member groups?
- Why might each group join?
- What does each group need?
- What can each group contribute?
- What barriers could prevent participation?
- What different levels of participation can we offer?
- What assumptions do we still need to validate?

If you cannot answer these questions, speak to potential members before moving forward.

---

# Your output

Update your **Community Blueprint** with:

- member groups

- participation pathways
- member personas

These will become the foundation for designing your community's activities, communication and engagement strategy.
