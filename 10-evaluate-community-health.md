# 10. Evaluate community health, value and impact

A Community of Practice should not be judged only by how many people belong to it or how many events it runs.

A healthy community creates value through:

- relationships
- learning
- knowledge exchange
- problem-solving
- collaboration
- development of practice
- new opportunities
- member leadership
- collective action

Evaluation helps you understand whether this is happening and where the community needs attention.

> **Measure what matters to the community — not simply what is easiest to count.**

---

# 10.1 Decide what you want to learn

Before choosing metrics, decide what you actually want to understand.

For example:

### Are people participating?

> 

### Are members learning from one another?

> 

### Are relationships developing?

> 

### Is the shared practice evolving?

> 

### Are members applying what they learn?

> 

### Is the community creating opportunities for collaboration?

> 

### Is the community influencing practice beyond its membership?

> 

### Is the community becoming more sustainable?

> 

---

# 10.2 Use three levels of evidence

A useful starting point is to distinguish between:

```text
ACTIVITY
    ↓
ENGAGEMENT
    ↓
OUTCOMES
```

## Level 1 — Activity

What did the community do?

Examples:

- number of meetings
- number of activities
- number of participants
- number of resources shared
- number of discussions
- number of working groups

These measures are useful for understanding what is happening.

But they do not tell you whether the activity created value.

---

## Level 2 — Engagement

What did people actually do?

Examples:

- members asking questions
- members answering one another
- members sharing experience
- members returning to activities
- members introducing others
- members organising activities
- members collaborating
- members taking leadership roles

Engagement measures tell you more about whether a community is becoming active.

---

## Level 3 — Outcomes

What changed because of the community?

Examples:

- members changed their practice
- members solved a problem
- members developed new skills
- members started a collaboration
- members accessed a new opportunity
- members developed a new resource
- organisations adopted a new approach
- the community influenced a wider field

Outcomes are usually more meaningful than activity counts, but they are also harder to measure and attribute.

---

# 10.3 Don't confuse membership with community

A long membership list does not necessarily indicate a healthy community.

For example:

> 500 registered members  
> 5 people regularly interacting

may represent a much less active community than:

> 60 members  
> 30 people regularly helping one another

Track membership, but don't treat it as the main indicator of community health.

---

# 10.4 Measure participation patterns

Instead of asking only:

> "How many people attended?"

also ask:

- Who participates?
- How often do they participate?
- Are people returning?
- Are members contributing?
- Are different people contributing?
- Are new members becoming more involved?
- Are experienced members helping newer members?

Look for patterns over time.

# 10.5 Measure the quality of interaction

Some of the most valuable community activity may be invisible in basic analytics.

Look for evidence of:

- people asking for advice
- people sharing experience
- people solving problems together
- people making introductions
- people sharing useful resources
- people challenging assumptions constructively
- people discussing failures as well as successes
- people building on one another's ideas

These interactions indicate that the community is functioning as a learning environment.

# 10.6 Measure relationships

Relationships are often one of the most important products of a community.

Ask:

> **Are members developing useful connections with one another?**

Possible indicators include:

- members contacting one another outside organised activities
- members making introductions
- new collaborations emerging
- members seeking advice from one another
- members reporting stronger professional networks
- members working across institutional or geographical boundaries

Quantity alone is not enough.

Ask about quality too.

# 10.7 Measure learning and changes in practice

A key question is:

> **What are members doing differently because of the community?**

This could include:

- adopting a new method
- changing a teaching approach
- using a new tool
- improving a workflow
- changing how they solve a recurring problem
- sharing knowledge with colleagues
- mentoring others
- introducing a new practice within their institution

Ask members to describe specific examples.

# 10.8 Measure collective value

Some outcomes only become possible because people are connected.

Examples include:

- collaborative research
- joint funding applications
- shared training programmes
- shared resources
- coordinated advocacy
- cross-institutional projects
- new partnerships
- joint publications
- mentoring relationships
- collective responses to emerging challenges

Ask:

> **What became possible because these people were connected?**

This is often more informative than asking whether the community was "successful".

---

# 10.9 Look for unexpected outcomes

Not all valuable outcomes can be predicted when a community is created.

For example:

- two members discover complementary expertise
- a new research collaboration emerges
- someone finds a mentor
- a member gets invited to contribute to another project
- a shared problem reveals a gap in training
- members collectively develop a new resource
- the community identifies a new area of practice

Create space for members to report unexpected outcomes.

### What happened that we did not plan for?

> 

---

# 10.10 Use qualitative evidence

Numbers can tell you **what happened**.

Stories can help explain **why it mattered**.

Collect examples such as:

- member testimonials
- short case studies
- stories of changed practice
- examples of collaboration
- examples of problems solved
- examples of resources being reused

Ask members:

> **Can you give an example of something that happened because of this community that would probably not have happened otherwise?**

This question can generate powerful evidence.

---

# 10.11 Build an evidence chain

When possible, connect:

```text
Community activity
        ↓
Member interaction
        ↓
Learning / relationship
        ↓
Change in practice
        ↓
Wider outcome
```

For example:

```text
Peer problem-solving session
        ↓
Two researchers share approaches
        ↓
Member learns a new workflow
        ↓
Workflow adopted in their laboratory
        ↓
Training resource developed for colleagues
```

The further you move along this chain, the more difficult attribution becomes.

Be transparent about this.

A community may contribute to an outcome without being its sole cause.

# 10.12 Combine quantitative and qualitative evidence

A useful evaluation does not need dozens of indicators.

A small balanced set is often better.

For example:

### Quantitative

- active members
- returning members
- member contributions
- member-led activities
- collaborations initiated

### Qualitative

- examples of changed practice
- member stories
- evidence of learning
- examples of useful relationships
- unexpected outcomes

# 10.13 Establish a baseline

If possible, collect some information before or at the beginning of the community.

For example:

- current collaborations
- current knowledge-sharing practices
- existing relationships
- members' confidence
- current training activity
- existing resources
- current challenges

You can then compare later findings against the starting point.

If you did not establish a baseline, don't assume evaluation is impossible.

You can still collect retrospective accounts and document changes over time.

---

# 10.14 Evaluate at different stages

Do not wait until the end of the year.

Consider evaluating at several points.

### Early stage

Ask:

- Are people participating?
- Are they finding value?
- Are relationships developing?

### Developing stage

Ask:

- Are members contributing?
- Is learning happening?
- Is responsibility becoming distributed?

### Established stage

Ask:

- Is practice changing?
- Are collaborations emerging?
- Is the community influencing its wider environment?

### Periodically

Ask:

> **Is this community still the right mechanism for the need it was created to address?**

Communities change.

The original purpose may no longer be relevant.

---

# 10.15 Use evaluation to make decisions

Evaluation should lead to action.

For example:

### Finding

Participation is declining.

### Possible response

Review activity formats and member needs.

---

### Finding

Many people attend but few contribute.

### Possible response

Create more participatory activities and smaller contribution opportunities.

---

### Finding

The same members do most of the work.

### Possible response

Create clearer pathways into facilitation and leadership.

---

### Finding

Members value relationships but formal events are poorly attended.

### Possible response

Create more informal and peer-led interaction.

---

### Finding

The community has achieved its original objective.

### Possible response

Redefine the domain, evolve the purpose or plan an intentional transition.

---

# 10.16 Don't use evaluation to control the community

Evaluation can become counterproductive when communities are judged only against externally imposed targets.

Remember:

- not everything valuable is immediately measurable
- relationship-building takes time
- learning may happen indirectly
- outcomes may emerge unexpectedly
- attribution is often complex
- communities evolve

Use evaluation to **learn and adapt**, not simply to prove that the community is busy.

# 10.18 Decide what happens next

After reviewing your evidence, choose an action.

### Continue

The community is creating value and should continue broadly as it is.

### Adapt

The community is valuable but something needs to change.

### Refocus

The original purpose is no longer sufficient.

### Pause

There is currently insufficient value or capacity to continue.

### Close

The community has fulfilled its purpose or is no longer the appropriate mechanism.

Closing a community can be a successful outcome.

Not every Community of Practice needs to exist indefinitely.

## Sources & evidence

### Primary source

Wenger-Trayner, E. & Wenger-Trayner, B. (2015). *Introduction to Communities of Practice: A Brief Overview of the Concept and its Uses.*

Wenger-Trayner and Wenger-Trayner explicitly challenge the idea that CoP impact is too difficult to measure. They note that while attribution may not be certain, quantitative and qualitative data can be combined to build a case for value and to trace changes in practice and performance.

Their examples of community activities also provide useful categories for identifying meaningful interaction, including problem-solving, seeking experience, reusing resources, coordination, discussing new developments, documenting learning and identifying gaps in competence.

### Additional source

Robinson, A. (2012). *The Less Visible Leader: Emerging Leadership Models for Environmental Networks, Coalitions, and Collaboratives*. Institute for Conservation Leadership.

Robinson highlights the importance of measuring the quality of relationships rather than simply their quantity, and describes network mapping and participation measures as possible sources of evidence.

### Our synthesis

The three-level evaluation model, Community Health Check, indicator-selection table, evidence-chain model and decision framework were developed specifically for this guide. They are intended as practical evaluation tools rather than prescribed CoP evaluation frameworks.
